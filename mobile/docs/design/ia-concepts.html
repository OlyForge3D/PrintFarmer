Five tabs, one hidden menu, and fourteen features with nowhere to live.

PrintFarmer's iPhone app serves two people equally: the operator walking the farm with a phone in one hand, and the owner checking whether the farm is healthy. Right now both of them share one structure that was designed for neither. Four ways to fix it, built and tappable.

The structural fault, stated plainly. An overflow menu that belongs to one screen is being used as the app's second navigation layer. Leave that tab and the entire oversight half of the product vanishes. Every concept below starts by deleting that menu.
Two contrast defects found while building these. pfError #dc2626 is identical in both themes and measures 3.9:1 as text on the dark navy background — under the 4.5:1 floor. pfWarning #d97706 measures 3.2:1 as text on white in light mode. Both are fine as filled badges with white text; both fail as label text. The mockups use lifted variants (#f87171 / #b45309) following the same adaptive pattern pfSuccess already uses. Worth a separate fix regardless of which concept wins.

A · Two Hats

The app has two jobs. Stop pretending it has one. A mode control in the navigation bar swaps the entire shell between Floor and Oversight: five complete destinations each, nothing in an overflow.

Tap Floor / Oversight in the nav bar. Both tab bars are live.

Where everything lives

Floor tabsAttention, Farm, Tasks, Scan, Inventory, exactly as they are today
Oversight tabsOverview · Fleet · Jobs · Upkeep · Reports
OverviewDashboard + Dispatch Dashboard, merged
JobsJob History, Job Timeline, job analytics
UpkeepMaintenance, Maintenance Analytics, Predictive Insights
ReportsUptime & Reliability, filament coverage rollups, Locations
Both modesAccount button, same corner, always: Notifications, Settings, Manage Servers, sign out
Server switcherOne fixed position in every mode and on every screen

Behaviour that matters

The mode is remembered per device, but urgency crosses it: a critical failure raises the badge in both tab bars, and tapping it from Oversight switches modes and lands on the item. A mode is a lens, never a wall.

On iPad the mode control disappears entirely; the sidebar shows both groups at once under Floor and Oversight headers, which is what the extra width is for.

When capability flags disable a destination, that mode simply has four tabs. If a mode drops below two, it collapses and its survivors merge into the other.

What it buys

  • Every orphaned destination gets a real, top-level home.
  • The two-audience truth is stated in the interface instead of hidden in it.
  • Smallest possible change to the floor workflow — operators lose nothing.
  • Cheapest to build: it is a second TabView, not a new interaction model.

What it costs

  • Ten destinations to learn instead of five.
  • Modes strand people. Someone will look for Job History in Floor mode and conclude it is gone.
  • The solo maker who is both audiences at once flips modes all day.
  • Deep links must carry their mode or land on the wrong shell.

A′ · Two Hats, adaptive

Two Hats, except the second hat only appears when there is a second person. The app reads what the server already knows and ships the shell that matches. One operator gets one shell with no mode to be lost in. A staffed farm gets the mode. Same destinations either way, so growing is an expansion rather than a migration.

Starts in the solo shell: four tabs, Oversight as a tab rather than a mode, and no scanner in the tab bar. Open Inventory to find it.

What it derives from, all of which already exists

shiftPlanEnabledThe server capability flag that literally means "this farm runs shifts". Already gates the Tasks tab
Account countGET /api/users — one account is a solo shop
Rolefarm_admin decides whether this person should see Oversight at all
LocationsOne bay is a workshop. Three bays is a farm
OverrideSettings → Navigation. Automatic, Simple, or Two modes. Never asked during onboarding

Five tabs at both scales, and the swap is the trick

SoloAttention · Farm · Inventory · Oversight
Farm, floorAttention · Farm · Tasks · Inventory
Farm, oversightOverview · Fleet · Jobs · Upkeep · Reports

A solo shop has shift planning off, so Tasks is already gone. That freed slot is exactly where Oversight goes. Neither shell is crowded, neither is padded, and nobody had to answer a question to get the right one.

Scan is not a place, so it stopped being a tab

Scanning is an input method. Spending a fifth of the most valuable navigation in the app on one is the same mistake as putting a keyboard in the tab bar. It now lives in three places instead, all of them where the work already is:

In the taskThe harvest item's primary action is Scan bin. Full-width, because that task genuinely is scanning. The operator never navigates to a scanner and then hunts for the job
InventoryOne icon in the navigation bar, where it stays put while the list scrolls
Outside the appAction Button, Control Center, and Lock Screen. For gloved hands this beats any in-app button

One affordance per context. An earlier pass of this mockup put a navigation-bar scan icon and a full-width scan button on the same Inventory screen: two controls, one action, one screen. That is what removing a tab does to your nerve. The prominent button belongs to a task that is actually about scanning; the screen gets the icon, which also survives scrolling.

One caution on "put it in Inventory": today's ScanView also drives the harvest flow, bin scanning, printer lookup, and the offline queue. Those are Farm and Attention jobs, not inventory jobs. Inventory earns the persistent entry for intake; the rest have to be reachable from the task, or the highest-frequency floor use gets harder rather than easier.

Dropping it leaves four tabs on the floor. Bigger targets, less to scan, and room for Oversight without the shell feeling full.

Persistent chrome, stated as a rule

The original diagnosis was that the server switcher sits top-leading on Attention and top-trailing on Farm. Fixing that needs a rule rather than a habit, because a habit is how it drifted in the first place, and an earlier pass of this very mockup dropped the account button from Farm and stranded the Oversight screens with no way back to Floor.

LeadingServer chip. Every tab root, identical position, because every screen shows one farm's data and you must always know which
TrailingAccount, always last. Screen actions such as filter, search, or scan sit to its left
PinnedThe mode control, on every root of both modes, so no screen is a dead end
Pushed screensNone of it. Back button and title take over, which is why Navigation has neither

Account survives in the navigation bar for the reason Attention does not: it is low-frequency and terminal, which is exactly the profile iOS puts top-right on a tab root in App Store, Music, and Photos. Attention is high-frequency, badged, and deep. Same corner, opposite jobs.

Why Attention stays in the tab bar

Moving it up between the server chip and the account button is tempting for the same reason it is risky: it is the move that produced the bug we started with. A navigation bar belongs to the screen you are on. Put a top-level destination there and it either has to be duplicated on every screen, or it vanishes the moment you leave, which is precisely how Dashboard, Maintenance, and Settings ended up unreachable behind one screen's overflow menu.

Three more things break. Attention is a destination with its own feed, filters, and detail navigation, and iOS reserves navigation bars for actions on the current screen. The badge would shrink from a system-standard, always-visible tab badge to a non-standard one on a small glyph. And Attention is the floor operator's most frequent destination, so it would move from the easiest one-handed reach on the phone to the hardest.

If the goal is "see alerts from anywhere", the tab badge already does that on every screen in the app. The nav bar would make it less visible, not more.

Why onboarding is the wrong place to ask

At first run the operator has never used the app, so "how should the interface accommodate you" is unanswerable. The fact underneath it, how big this operation is, the server already knows more accurately than any self-report. And an answer captured once during setup is wrong permanently: the one-person shop that hires someone never reopens onboarding.

So the upgrade arrives in context and once. When the farm crosses a threshold, a second account, a second bay, shift planning switched on, Oversight offers to become its own mode. One tap, dismissible, reversible in Settings.

Two different things are called "advanced"

The brief was that features are hard to find, so an opt-in that hides features would rebuild the overflow menu as a preference pane. The split has to be honest:

SecondaryJob History, Timeline, Uptime, Analytics. Never hidden. The layout changes their prominence, never their existence
DangerousJog, preheat, home, z-offset, disable motors. A real opt-in, because misuse damages hardware or a running print

Only the second one earns a switch. Conflating them is how a product ends up gating the manual and shipping the loaded gun.

What it buys

  • The common case, one owner-operator, never sees a mode at all.
  • Correct by default with zero questions asked, and self-correcting as the farm changes.
  • All fourteen orphans still get a home in both shells.
  • Growth expands the same structure instead of relocating it.

What it costs

  • Two shells to build, test, and screenshot, plus the threshold logic between them.
  • Derivation can be wrong. A solo owner running a 40-printer farm reads as staffed.
  • Needs an account-count endpoint the mobile client does not call today.
  • Anything automatic needs a visible, plain-language override or it feels arbitrary.

B · Gate Board

A farm is a departures board. One ranked, live list of everything the farm needs, ordered by when it needs it. Rows rerank in place and hold their alert until someone has actually seen them. Tapping a row hands you a pass: one screen carrying the whole task, scanner included.

Tap any row to open its pass. The simulate button is a mockup control, not part of the design.

Where everything lives

TabsBoard · Fleet · Inventory · Reports · You
BoardAttention, Tasks, harvests, runouts, maintenance due, as one ranked list filtered by lane
LanesAll · Now · Printers · Filament · Parts · Upkeep
PassThe full task: countdown, one primary action, the scanner, the camera, the history
FleetPrinter list, detail, advanced controls, locations
ReportsDashboard, Dispatch, Job History, Timeline, Uptime, Maintenance Analytics, Predictive
YouNotifications, Settings, Manage Servers, Offline Queue, account

The one authored moment

When a printer's state changes, its row does not disappear and come back somewhere else. It travels to its new rank, keeps its identity, and holds an amber ring until it is opened. Rank changes silently all day; a change you need to know about announces itself and then waits. Reduce Motion swaps the travel for a held flash, never for a silent jump.

Scan stops being a tab. It becomes what it actually is: an input method that appears inside the task that needs it. The operator scans a bin from the harvest pass instead of navigating to a scanner and then finding the job.

What it buys

  • The operator stops navigating. Work arrives in order.
  • One structure serves both audiences: the board is the floor, Reports is the office.
  • Change is impossible to miss and impossible to miss twice.
  • Capability gating is free: a disabled feature contributes no rows.

What it costs

  • Only as good as its ranking. A bad rank order turns the board into noise.
  • Needs real server work: a unified, ranked, cross-domain feed endpoint.
  • "Where is the printer list" becomes a genuine question for the first week.
  • Deferring something you have seen needs a real snooze model or the board nags.

C · Fleet Spine

Printers are the noun this product is about, so the fleet is home. And nothing is ever hidden again, because every destination shows you what is inside it before you go there. You cannot fail to discover a feature that is currently telling you it has four things waiting.

Tap any live window to open it. More is the full gallery.

Where everything lives

TabsFleet · Attention · Scan · Inventory · More
FleetPrinter cards, then a grid of live windows into every other destination
Live windowName, current count, and the two or three actual rows inside it
MoreEvery remaining destination, each one showing its live contents
Identity chipServer name in the same header position on all five tabs
Printer detailControls, Advanced, Predictive, history, reached through its printer where it belongs

Why the preview is the whole idea

Discoverability is not a labelling problem. Operators do not open Maintenance to find out whether maintenance is due; they open it because they already suspect it is. A window that reads 2 due · Voron-02 nozzle, X1C-04 belt answers the question without the trip, and earns the trip when the answer is interesting.

This also makes capability flags graceful: a disabled feature's window simply is not in the grid. No empty tab, no placeholder, no explanation needed.

The cost is honest: every window is a fetch. This needs cheap summary endpoints returning counts and a two-row preview, or the home screen becomes the slowest screen in the app.

What it buys

  • Discovery by evidence rather than by memorising a menu.
  • The fleet — the thing both audiences actually care about — leads.
  • Familiar: it is still five tabs, so nothing about today's muscle memory breaks.
  • Degrades gracefully to plain rows when the summaries fail.

What it costs

  • New backend surface: cheap per-feature summary endpoints.
  • A long home screen. Scrolling past the fleet to reach the windows is real friction.
  • Oversight is present but never foregrounded; the owner still starts on the floor.
  • Live previews plus SignalR is a real performance budget, not a free one.

D · Workbench

Two audiences, one app, so stop choosing. Home is a stack of panels each person composes themselves, drawn from a complete, readable catalogue of everything the app can do. The catalogue is the discoverability fix: you find features by reading the list of features.

Tap Edit to open the catalogue, or the Search tab.

Where everything lives

TabsWorkbench · Fleet · Scan · Search · You
WorkbenchPinned, reorderable panels. Ships with a Floor preset and an Oversight preset
CatalogueEvery destination, grouped, each with one line saying what it is for
SearchA real tab. Printers, spools, jobs, parts, locations, and settings by name
YouNotifications, Settings, Manage Servers, Offline Queue, account
PanelsAttention, Ready to harvest, Low spools, Maintenance due, Fleet strip, Dispatch queue, Today's jobs, Uptime

Why a catalogue beats a menu

An overflow menu is a list of names. A catalogue is a list of names with a sentence explaining what each one does, and it is searchable. "Predictive Insights" means nothing in a menu; "Which printers are trending toward a failure" is findable. This is the only concept where a feature can be discovered by someone who does not already know it exists.

Search as a tab is the escape hatch that makes the whole app addressable: type z-offset, get Advanced Controls; type a spool colour, get the spool; type uptime, get the report.

The risk is first run. An unconfigured workbench is the worst screen in any of these four concepts, so the presets are not a nicety. They are load-bearing.

What it buys

  • Serves both audiences without a mode, and serves the person who is both.
  • The catalogue is a permanent, self-updating answer to "what can this app do".
  • Search makes every destination reachable in two taps regardless of layout.
  • New features ship into the catalogue instead of into an overflow menu.

What it costs

  • By far the most engineering: a panel system, persistence, reordering, presets.
  • Configurability is a tax on people who just want it to work.
  • Two users' apps no longer look alike — support and documentation get harder.
  • Panel layout must survive capability flags turning a pinned panel off.

What to do with this

A′ is the recommendation. It keeps Two Hats' structure, which is what makes every orphaned destination reachable, and removes the one thing that hurts your most common user: a mode that a single owner-operator has to flip all day. The staffed farm still gets the mode when the server says it is a staffed farm.

The other three are not runners-up so much as different bets. Averaging any of them together produces the app that exists today. Pick the one whose cost you are willing to pay, and the rest of the design follows from it.
